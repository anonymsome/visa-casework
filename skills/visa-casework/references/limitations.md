# What this doesn't know, and what you should confirm yourself

Read this before relying on anything in `references/`. Some of it was
verified against primary sources on a specific date; some of it was not; all
of it goes stale.

## What success looks like

Worth being blunt about, because the rest of this page only makes sense
against it.

**This does not get you a visa appointment.** Nothing in it can. Appointment
supply is a function of consular staffing and vetting requirements, and no
congressional office controls either.

What it does is get your situation in front of a person whose job is to ask
the agency questions, and get you a real answer instead of a portal that
never loads. Three things that are actually achievable:

- **You're heard.** A named caseworker knows your situation exists.
- **You get an answer.** Often just "no availability, no timeline" — but from
  a human, in writing, instead of inferred from a screen showing `NA`.
- **You're counted.** Offices track what constituents contact them about.
  Volume on an issue is how it reaches the people who set policy. One letter
  is one data point; it is not nothing.

Anything beyond that — an appointment, an expedite, a changed policy — is
possible but is not what you should expect, and no draft this produces should
imply otherwise. If a user walks away expecting a visa, the tool has
misinformed them, however good the letter was.

## What the agent cannot know

- **The user's full immigration record.** You know what they told you. The
  concern check in `references/is-it-safe.md` screens what was said, not
  what exists in a file at USCIS or State.
- **Their employer's filings.** Whether a petition was amended, withdrawn, or
  is under investigation is not visible from an approval notice.
- **Current policy.** Consular policy, expedite criteria, third-country
  rules and vetting requirements have all changed more than once in the last
  year. Anything here has a shelf life measured in months.
- **What a specific office will do.** Offices differ in process, speed, and
  appetite. Two verified examples do not generalize.

## What the user should confirm themselves

Say this plainly rather than burying it. In rough priority order:

1. **Their own dates, from their own documents.** Petition validity, status
   start, I-94 expiry, OPT end. Users misremember these routinely — one
   confidently stated a status date in this tool's own testing that was
   nowhere in their approval notice. Read the paperwork; do not trust memory,
   including your own agent-side summary of it.
2. **Current appointment and expedite policy**, at travel.state.gov and at
   their own post's site. Any policy sentence in a letter should be checked
   the week it is sent.
3. **Their congressional district**, via the official House lookup — ZIP is
   not always sufficient, and an office will not act for a non-constituent.
4. **Their office's actual process.** Whether the form is the whole thing,
   whether staff contact comes first, whether a signature page must be
   mailed. Getting this wrong means submitting and then waiting forever for
   an inquiry that never started.
5. **Anything in the letter they are not certain is true.** They sign it.
   An uncertain claim should be softened or cut, never left in because it
   sounded good.
6. **With an attorney, if they have one**: whether there is any reason not to
   file. One email. Cheapest risk reduction available.

## For contributors

If you have actually filed one of these, what happened is worth more than
anything in this directory. Corrections to the "not verified" rows are the
single most useful contribution, especially with a primary source and a date.

When you verify something, update the table above with the source and the
date you checked. A claim without a date is a claim that will silently rot.
