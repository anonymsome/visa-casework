# "Will reaching out put my status at risk?"

Most people in this situation never contact anyone. The reason is usually
not that they do not know the option exists — it is that they are afraid
asking will draw attention to them. Many carry that fear for months while
the problem gets worse.

**Raise this before the user has to.** Someone worried about being a target
will not volunteer the worry; they will just quietly not file. Say it out
loud in onboarding, in plain terms, and let them decide with real
information instead of dread.

This page is not legal advice, and none of it is a guarantee. It is the
honest shape of the thing.

## You are a constituent

The most common version of the fear is quieter than risk: *do I even have
standing to ask?*

Yes. **Congressional offices serve the residents of their district.
Constituency is about where you live, not whether you can vote.** You are
not a guest here petitioning for a favor. A member's office represents
everyone in the district — citizens, permanent residents, people on
temporary status — and immigration casework is one of the largest categories
of work those offices do. Yours would not be an unusual request. It would be
a Tuesday.

The right to petition the government runs to *persons*, not only to
citizens. Not voting does not remove it.

## What actually happens when you file

1. You submit the form and sign a Privacy Act release.
2. A caseworker reads it and, if it is in scope, sends an inquiry to the
   State Department's congressional liaison office.
3. The liaison asks the relevant post or bureau, and a response comes back
   to the caseworker.
4. The caseworker passes the answer to you.

It is a question, routed through an established channel that exists for this
purpose. It is **not** a referral to enforcement, not a complaint, not an
audit, and not a flag. The office has no authority to adjudicate your case
and no role in enforcement.

Congressional inquiries on immigration matters are routine and high-volume.
A caseworker who handles immigration sees these constantly.

## Run the check; do not hand over the worry

The default answer is **yes, it is okay to file.** For the large majority of
people in this situation nothing in their record is remotely unusual, and
saying so plainly is the job.

The failure mode to avoid is handing the user a general warning — "consult an
attorney if anything is complicated" — and leaving them to decide whether
they qualify. They cannot assess that, the ambiguity reads as a hint that
something might be wrong with them, and they file nothing. A caveat offered
to everyone is not caution; it is the thing that keeps people stuck.

So: **run the flag check yourself, and state the result out loud.**

### Flags that trigger an attorney referral

Refer only when one of these is actually present in what the user has told
you:

- Any period **out of status**, or an overstay, however brief.
- **Unauthorized work**, including work before an EAD or outside the terms of
  the petition.
- A **prior visa refusal**, a 221(g) that was never resolved, or a prior
  misrepresentation or fraud finding.
- **Removal or deportation proceedings**, past or present, or a prior order.
- A **criminal arrest or charge**, whatever the outcome.
- **Status expiring within a few months**, or already expired.
- A **dispute with the employer** about the petition — a terminated or
  amended petition, a job or worksite change never filed for, a pending
  investigation.
- **J-1 212(e)** two-year home-residency requirement unsatisfied and unwaived.
- The case belongs to **someone else**, or is entangled with another adult's.

### If no flag is present

Say so, specifically and without hedging. Name what you checked:

> Nothing you've described raises a flag. Your status is valid, you're
> working for the employer who petitioned for you, there's no gap and no
> prior refusal. This is a clean file and it's okay to submit.

A vague reassurance does not land. Naming the specific things that are fine
is what lets someone actually put the fear down.

### If a flag is present

Name the specific flag, say why it warrants a look, and be clear it is not a
verdict:

> One thing before you file: you mentioned a few weeks between your OPT
> ending and your H-1B starting. That's the kind of detail an immigration
> attorney should look at first — not because filing is dangerous, but
> because you should know what's in your own file before inviting someone to
> read it. Everything else here looks straightforward.

Do not refuse to continue. Draft the request, keep it for them, and let them
file it after they have spoken to counsel. The tool's job is to make them
ready, not to gatekeep.

### When the user already has a lawyer

Many do. Then the cheapest possible step is one email: *"any reason not to
file this?"* Recommend that instead of a general caution — it costs them a
day and it resolves the question properly.

## The honest caveats

Anyone who tells you there is zero risk is guessing. What is true:

- **An inquiry means a human at State looks at your file.** For almost
  everyone that is neutral or good — being looked at is the entire point,
  and nothing happens to files nobody looks at. But attention is not the
  same as no attention.
- **If something in your record is genuinely irregular** — unauthorized
  work, a period out of status, a prior refusal or misrepresentation, a
  dispute about your employer's filings — then **talk to an immigration
  attorney before filing anything.** Not because the inquiry is dangerous in
  itself, but because you should know what is in your own file before
  inviting a look at it. This is the one situation where the honest advice
  is to stop and get counsel.
- **The release is real.** You are authorizing the office to obtain and
  discuss your records with the agency. That is what makes the inquiry
  possible. See `references/privacy-act.md`.
- **An inquiry cannot change an adjudication**, so it cannot make a decision
  go against you either. It also cannot make one go for you. See
  `references/what-offices-can-do.md`.
- **Nothing here is a guarantee**, and this tool is not a lawyer.

## What goes in the draft, and what does not

**Include:**

- Your name, home address, phone, email — the office verifies district
  residency and needs to reach you.
- Your status category and the dates from your approval notice.
- Your employer or school, and the city.
- Your country of citizenship and which post you would use.
- What the scheduling system actually shows, and when you last checked.
- What you have already tried.
- The human facts: who you cannot reach, what has happened, how long it has
  been.

**Leave out:**

- A-number, date of birth, passport number, visa foil number, SSN, USCIS
  receipt numbers. The form collects these separately, typed by you. See
  the never-collect list in `references/intake.md`.
- **Legal conclusions about your own case.** Do not write "I believe I
  qualify for an expedite" or "I am eligible under X." State facts and let
  the office and the agency draw conclusions. A wrong self-assessment in
  writing is worse than none.
- **Speculation about your employer's filings** if you are not certain of
  the details. Say what your approval notice says and stop there.
- **Anything you are not sure is true.** Uncertain is fine to say out loud:
  "as I understand it" or "I have not been able to confirm."
- **Anger at the agency, the administration, or the system.** Understandable
  and counterproductive; it costs you credibility with the person who would
  be advocating for you.
- **Anything you are not comfortable having in a government file.** If you
  would not want it read aloud, leave it out. The letter can be honest
  without being complete.

## How to raise this with a user

Short, early, and without pressure. Something like:

> One thing people usually worry about and don't say: contacting your
> representative's office doesn't put your status at risk. You're a
> constituent because you live in the district — voting has nothing to do
> with it, and immigration cases are a large share of what these offices
> do. The office asks the State Department a question; it has no
> enforcement role. The one real exception is if something in your own
> record is irregular, in which case talk to an attorney first.

Then stop. Do not sell it. If the user is still uneasy, that is a legitimate
place to land — offer to leave the draft with them unsent, and note that
nothing is filed until they submit it themselves.
