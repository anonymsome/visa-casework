# Framing: why hardship beats a visa request

The single highest-leverage decision in the draft. Two requests with
identical facts get opposite handling depending on the ask.

## The ask that gets declined

> I am writing to ask for your help in getting my visa approved.

A congressional office cannot approve a visa, direct a consular officer, or
overturn a refusal. Consular nonreviewability means even the State
Department's own headquarters does not overrule an officer's decision on the
merits. An office receiving this request has one honest answer: we cannot do
that. The case closes having consumed the user's one clean approach.

## The ask that gets worked

> I am a constituent lawfully present and working in the United States. I am
> functionally unable to leave the country and return, because there is no
> available appointment at the post I would use. I am asking your office to
> inquire with the Department of State about appointment availability, and
> to ask whether my circumstances warrant expedited consideration.

This is inside scope. The office opens a congressional inquiry with State's
Bureau of Consular Affairs, which has a channel for exactly this. The user
gets a real answer from a person instead of a portal that never loads.

## Why the distinction is real and not a wording trick

The difference is not phrasing — it is *what is being asked of whom*.

- Adjudication is the consular officer's, and it is insulated from outside
  influence by design. Asking an office to touch it asks them to do
  something improper, and they know it.
- Appointment availability, processing status, and expedite eligibility are
  administrative. They are legitimately inquirable, they are where cases
  actually get stuck, and an inquiry from a member's office reaches a human
  who can look.

Do not coach the user to disguise the first as the second. If what they
want is a refusal reversed, the honest answer is that this tool cannot help
and an attorney is the right call.

## The strongest available argument: no reachable remedy

Where the facts support it, this is the argument that does the most work,
because it explains why a congressional inquiry is the *appropriate* tool
rather than a shortcut around one.

The published remedies are unreachable by their own design:

- **Expedite requires an existing appointment to expedite.** An applicant
  with no booked appointment has nothing to apply the expedite to. The
  remedy the State Department publishes for urgent cases is structurally
  unavailable to exactly the people with no slots.
- **Rescheduling is rationed.** Accounts are limited in how many times they
  may reschedule, so an applicant holding a distant appointment cannot
  safely gamble it against a possible earlier one — losing the reschedule
  budget can leave them with nothing.
- **Third-country processing was closed.** As of roughly September 2025,
  applicants must apply in their country of nationality or residence, which
  removed the historical pressure valve and concentrated demand.

Stated together: *there is no step I can take myself.* That is precisely the
situation casework exists for, and it distinguishes the request from someone
who simply wants to skip a line. Say it plainly, in one short paragraph,
without self-pity.

Check each element against the user's actual facts before using it. An
applicant who does hold an appointment cannot claim the first point.

## Structure that works

Caseworkers read many of these. Two tight paragraphs of specific fact beat a
page of formality.

1. **Who and where.** Constituent, district, status category, how long in
   the U.S., current employer or school. Establishes standing immediately.
2. **The blockage, concretely.** Which post, what the wait is, what has been
   tried. Specific and checkable.
3. **What is on the other side.** The reason travel matters — the sick
   parent, the funeral, the dependent abroad. Names and dates, not
   adjectives.
4. **The ask.** Inquiry, and expedite consideration where supported.
5. **Availability.** Phone, email, willingness to sign the release.

## What weakens a request

- Legal argument. The office is not adjudicating; briefing them reads as a
  misunderstanding of what they do.
- Anger at the State Department. Understandable, and it costs credibility
  with the person who has to advocate for you.
- Vagueness about the hardship. "Family emergency" does nothing. "My mother
  had a stroke in March and I have not been able to visit" does.
- Inflated or invented hardship. Caseworkers read these all day and
  calibrate. A fabrication that gets noticed burns the user's credibility
  and, cumulatively, everyone else's.
- A weak expedite ask stapled to a sound inquiry. It drags the whole thing
  toward "no."
